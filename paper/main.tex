  A MATE-Grounded Evaluation with Strict Measurement}
\author{Vedang Pandey}

\begin{abstract}
Chess is a standard testbed for reasoning and instruction-following in
language models, but evaluations rarely measure the newest small models,
and the answers reported by models are not always the answers scored. We
build on MATE~\cite{wang2024mate}, which fine-tuned an 8B model on one
million expert-annotated chess positions and beat GPT/Claude/Gemini at
move selection, and update it to current hardware: 4-bit LoRA fine-tuning
of Gemma~4 E2B/E4B, compared against DeepSeek V4 Flash on MATE's held-out
move-selection set and on standard-chess mate-in-1/2 and Stockfish
best-move batteries. This draft reports the measurement infrastructure and
the first honest results: a Phase~1 representation study that motivates
FEN-only prompts, and a 1000-position DeepSeek run on MATE selection that
scored 48.6\% (chance on a balanced binary task) while exhibiting a strong
position-independent preference for the second candidate. We also report
a measured failure mode of thinking-mode inference under bounded token
budgets, and detail the strict, regression-tested answer extraction that
guarantees every scored answer is the model's own text.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
On-device agents are increasingly small language models (SLMs), and
games with explicit rules and oracles are a clean probe of their
reasoning. Existing chess benchmarks~\cite{ruoss2024chessbench,kolasani2025llmchess,wen2025chessqa}
evaluate frontier-scale models; the training methods that close the gap
between small models and frontiers at chess are either large-scale and
unreproducible~\cite{feng2023chessgpt} or not yet validated on the latest
architectures. Our project asks one question:

\begin{quote}
Can a current on-device model (Gemma~4 E2B, 2B), fine-tuned on
expert-annotated chess data via 4-bit LoRA, close the gap to a frontier
reasoning model (DeepSeek V4 Flash) at chess?
\end{quote}

This draft is a working paper. It reports the evaluation infrastructure,
the datasets, and the first measured results, and it is honest about what
is not yet known.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{MATE}~\cite{wang2024mate} collects one million positions annotated
by expert chess players (including world champions) for strategy and
tactic, with and without explanations, and fine-tunes LLaMA-3-8B to select
the better of two candidate moves; the fine-tune outperforms GPT-4o,
Claude, and Gemini. We adopt MATE as the primary base: its recipe
(fine-tune, then compare against frontiers on a held-out test set) is our
recipe, updated to Gemma~4 and DeepSeek V4 Flash.

\textbf{KinGPT}~\cite{tang2026gambit} is a 25M character-level model
trained only on (position, best-move) pairs that \emph{outperforms} much
larger chess LLMs on a mate-in-N suite, while failing to generalize ---
strong evidence that puzzle accuracy alone does not imply chess
understanding, and that training/evaluation FEN overlap must be audited.
We adopt its evaluation hygiene: theme-held-out splits, position-vs-puzzle
accuracy, legality as a separate metric, and pass@1 as the default.

\textbf{Other benchmarks.} ChessQA~\cite{wen2025chessqa} organizes chess
understanding into five ability categories (structural, motifs, short
tactics, position judgment, semantic); our task battery maps onto short
tactics and position judgment, with the categories as future scope.
LLM CHESS~\cite{kolasani2025llmchess} provides game-play protocols;
ChessBench~\cite{ruoss2024chessbench} is the dataset ancestor of our
lichess-derived sets.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Models}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{gemma4-e2b / gemma4-e4b} (Google, 2B/4B): local 4-bit
  inference on a Kaggle T4. Thinking disabled at load time.
  Fine-tuned variants (MATE-LoRA, 4-bit QLoRA) are the central planned
  intervention.
\item \textbf{deepseek-v4-flash} (frontier, via the opencode-go gateway):
  evaluated in two modes --- thinking enabled and thinking disabled ---
  because, as we measure in \S\ref{sec:mate}, thinking-mode inference does
  not complete within practical token budgets.
\end{itemize}
All models run with a \textbf{2048-token generation budget}, identical
across models, so token economics are comparable.

\subsection{Rules and representation}
All positions and oracles use \emph{standard} chess (castling, en passant,
double-step, promotion), enforced with python-chess. Every position is
presented in FEN notation, chosen on the basis of the Phase~1 study
(\S\ref{sec:phase1}).

\subsection{Answer extraction is strict and never invents answers}
Each sample records the model's exact output text. The scored move is
extracted in a fixed, regression-tested priority order:
(1) the last \texttt{MOVE:}-prefixed from-to token (the prompt's demanded
format); (2) a line that is exactly a from-to move; (3) SAN resolution via
python-chess, which by construction can only return legal moves.
There is \textbf{no fallback}: no forced second pass, no extraction from
the reasoning text, no random choice. If the model returns nothing
parseable, the sample is a \texttt{parse\_error}/\texttt{no\_answer}.
Every recorded answer is the model's own verbatim text; the residual risk
(adversarial phrasing such as ``not MOVE: e2e4'') is documented and
mitigated by the prompt's output specification.

\subsection{Token and response accounting}
Every sample stores prompt, model input, full thinking text (when
produced), final answer, oracle, verdict, and a normalized usage record:
input/output/reasoning/total tokens, cache reads/misses, time to first
token, generation latency, and token rates. Missing provider fields
remain missing rather than imputed. These records drive the paper's
figures: performance-versus-tokens, outcome breakdowns by category, and a
category-by-model accuracy heatmap.

\section{Phase 1: representation study}
\label{sec:phase1}
Before the main sweep we measured how board representation affects one
model's (deepseek-v4-flash) ability to solve mate-in-1 positions under
standard chess. Twenty positions (5 per representation) were presented as
a rendered grid, FEN, per-piece bitboards, and a piece list
(Table~\ref{tab:phase1}). FEN was solved perfectly, grid and list
partially, and bitboards not at all --- including two illegal moves, the
only illegal moves in the study. The main evaluation therefore uses FEN
only. \emph{Caveat: this is $n{=}5$ per representation --- directional
evidence, not statistics.}

\section{MATE move-selection results}
\label{sec:mate}
We benchmarked deepseek-v4-flash on 1000 positions from MATE's held-out
strategy test set: FEN + two candidate moves with expert explanations;
the model must output \texttt{MoveA:\textless move\textgreater} or
\texttt{MoveB:\textless move\textgreater}; scored against the expert
choice. Direct mode, 2048-token budget.

\paragraph{Result.} \textbf{48.6\% accuracy} (486/1000) --- statistically
indistinguishable from chance on a balanced binary task, despite a 100\%
parse rate (every position answered natively, 2.1s average latency,
$\sim$6 output tokens). The failure is not uniform noise: the model
selected candidate B in 68\% of positions, and conditional accuracy is
31\% when the expert truth is A versus 67\% when it is B
(Table~\ref{tab:mate}).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\hline
& Accuracy & $n$ \\ \hline
Overall & 48.6\% & 1000 \\
Truth = A & 31\% & 506 \\
Truth = B & 67\% & 494 \\
Model picks A & 51\% & 322 \\
Model picks B & 51\% & 678 \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{MATE move selection, deepseek-v4-flash, direct mode, 2048
budget. Truth is balanced; the model is strongly B-biased.}
\label{tab:mate}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Interpretation, stated precisely.} Direct-mode deepseek does
not solve MATE selection above chance and exhibits a strong
position-independent preference for the second candidate. \emph{We do not
know why it prefers B.} Candidate order (MoveB is always listed second),
content differences in explanations, or direct-mode behavior are all
plausible; an order-randomization experiment (swapping MoveA/MoveB in the
prompt) is the planned test. We explicitly avoid claiming the cause.

\paragraph{A measured failure mode of thinking-mode inference.} With
thinking enabled, deepseek-v4-flash did not produce a single native answer
within 2048-8192 token budgets (0/10 at 2048 total), consuming the whole
budget on reasoning; at a 16k budget it answered natively on 1/3 positions
(3.5k reasoning tokens) and failed on the others (16k+ tokens, empty
content). With thinking disabled it answered 10/10 at $\sim$170 tokens
with 80\% accuracy on a 10-position pilot. This is a real, reproducible
model-plus-gateway behavior: thinking-mode reasoning is highly
token-hungry and does not reliably terminate within practical budgets on
this task. It motivates the direct-mode default and makes the
thinking-vs-direct accuracy gap itself a planned measurement.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Direct-mode ceiling.} The 48.6\% result is for direct-mode
  inference at a 2048 budget, the budget chosen for gemma parity. A
  thinking-on subset (16k budget) and the fine-tuned gemma are the
  planned comparisons; both may change the picture.
\item \textbf{The B-preference is unexplained.} Order-randomization is the
  immediate next experiment.
\item \textbf{Phase 1 is small.} $n{=}5$ per representation.
\item \textbf{Gateway constraints.} The opencode-go gateway serializes
  heavy generations per API key and enforces thinking budgets loosely;
  these are documented in the repository rather than hidden.
\item \textbf{Future work.} Gemma 4 E2B 4-bit LoRA on 50k MATE examples;
  evaluation of base vs LoRA vs deepseek on the MATE test set and the
  tactical battery; a 100-game head-to-head with full per-move telemetry;
  candidate-order randomization as a standard control.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
This draft reports the measurement stack and the first honest results of
a MATE-grounded comparison between fine-tuned SLMs and a frontier model at
chess. The infrastructure guarantees that every scored answer is the
model's own output, with strict extraction, standard chess, and complete
token accounting. The first measured result --- chance-level MATE
selection with a strong unexplained B-preference --- is a finding, not a
failure of the benchmark: it is precisely the kind of behavior the study
exists to measure.
